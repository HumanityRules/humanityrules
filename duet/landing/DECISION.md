# The landing page decision

You are choosing between two finished page designs. Six yes/no questions decide it. Answer them honestly; a yes binds how you actually operate, and any no simply selects the second page, which is also good and is buildable today.

## Page 1: The Design-Partner Compact — if you answer yes to all six

The idea: a small company that publishes who it can serve, who it cannot, and what each side owes the other, and visibly refuses money accordingly, reads as trustworthy in a way no marketing can fake.

What a visitor sees, top to bottom:

1. One sentence saying what Humanity Rules is. "Built and run by Victor Mendiluce." Two screenshots: your real agent's card in the control plane, then that same agent at the same URL doing one small real task (adding a row to a real spreadsheet and reading it back).
2. All four plans with exact prices. Trial and Operator are ordinary hosted plans anyone can get. Team and Enterprise say: through the design-partner program below.
3. Who the program serves now, and who should not apply yet. Your capacity stated as fact ("I can support at most N customer-cloud partners while operating solo, and I onboard one at a time"), then two published lists: "Team is for you now if..." and "Please don't apply yet if...".
4. Before you apply: four blunt facts. Deployment requires a role with AdministratorAccess. Integration credentials are stored in HumR's database. The agent, its files, and its history live in your AWS account. The control plane and parts of the runtime depend on HumR. Plus: "No external organization runs a HumR agent in its own AWS account yet. This program exists to change that, one partner at a time."
5. The compact itself: what a partner can hold you to (response window, working sessions, incident terms, exit help) and what you hold them to (a named infrastructure owner, a real workflow, feedback). Exact partner pricing and the right to leave without converting.
6. When the program ends: applications close at the Nth partner or a fixed date, whichever comes first. The page is then replaced, and the replacement publishes how many partners you onboarded, even if the number is zero.
7. You: name, face, Millbrae, solo, self-funded, pre-beta.
8. A short application form matching the published criteria exactly. You personally review every application and answer within Y days with one of four outcomes: accepted, declined with the reason, redirected to a hosted plan, or not yet.
9. A plain footer.

## Page 2: The Plain Product Card — if any answer is no

The idea: refuse spectacle, not information. A dense, exact page that reads like a well-made product datasheet. No selling. The precision is the trust signal.

What a visitor sees, top to bottom:

1. One sentence saying what Humanity Rules is, a Sign up button, and the same two screenshots.
2. Four product facts, stated plainly: the agent runs in its own cloud runtime and keeps working when no one is connected; members connect their own provider logins (admins can also share one); Trial and Operator run in HumR's cloud, Team and Enterprise in the customer's AWS account; built on the open-source Hermes Agent.
3. The four-plan pricing grid. Team's price cell reads "$29/agent/month · AWS infrastructure and model usage are separate."
4. You: name, face, Millbrae, "Solo founder. Self-funded, with no venture capital," and a short personal statement in your own recorded words.
5. Stage, stated flat: "HumR is pre-beta. Seven friends-and-family agent deployments are live. No external organization runs HumR in its own AWS account today." Then one action: Email about Team.
6. A plain footer.

This page is fully specified and ready to build now. Your only work is assets: a portrait, a recorded personal statement, two screenshots, and final fact-checks at build time.

## The six questions

A yes means: I authorize this statement with every blank filled in, and I will operate this way. A yes with a blank left open is a no. Any single no means Page 2.

**Q1 — Scope.** Will you publish and honor: "Trial and Operator are hosted paths and are never subject to design-partner fit selection. Every customer-cloud deployment, including Enterprise, enters through the same design-partner selection process. A larger contract does not bypass the published refusal criteria." — even when a big check shows up?

**Q2 — Capacity.** Fill in N and publish: "I can support at most [N] customer-cloud partners at the promised cadence while operating solo, and I onboard only one at a time." Is that true of your actual hours, not chosen to look scarce?

**Q3 — Refusal that costs money.** Will you publish a "please don't apply yet if..." list that would exclude some plausible paying applicants (for example: no named infrastructure owner; no concrete workflow you'll actually exercise), and enforce it by declining people who fail it?

**Q4 — Obligations.** Fill in every blank and publish both lists: your response window of [X business days], [cadence] of working sessions, incident terms, exit work; their named owner, exercised workflow, feedback cadence. Plus: you personally review every application and respond within [Y business days], naming the failed criterion when you decline. Will you be held to each line?

**Q5 — Economics.** Fill in and publish the partner pricing or discount, the program duration, what happens at program end at the published $29/agent/month Team price, and the right to leave without converting. Nothing may read "contact me for terms."

**Q6 — The end of the program.** Fill in N and a date, and publish: applications close at the earlier of the two; the page is then replaced (Team opens normally, or is paused or withdrawn, or a new bounded offer replaces it); the replacement states how many partners this program onboarded, including zero; the program does not renew. Will you execute that when the trigger hits?
